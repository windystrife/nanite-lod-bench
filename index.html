Unreal Engine 5.8 · Radeon 680M handheld · 1280×800

Nanite vs LOD

A replication of CRDG Studio's Nanite vs LOD test (UE 5.5, RX 580) on the Unreal Engine 5.8.4 build of a shipping game, measured on an AOKZOE A1 handheld (Radeon 680M, RDNA 2, 12 CU) at 1280×800. The same Megascans Wooden Table is placed 1, 2048, 4096 and 8192 times as separate static mesh actors and seen from eight camera views, lit and unlit.

Two meshes, one per side: Nanite uses the raw scan (2 000 018 triangles). LOD is a classic non-Nanite mesh built from the Megascans High, Mid and Low tiers with generated LODs in between (8 LODs, LOD0 21 852 triangles). Both are shadowed by cascaded shadow maps, no virtual shadow maps, TAA at native resolution.

Verdict

From 2048 copies on, Nanite is faster in every view that has tables in it (the one exception, the close view at 2048, is a tie at 0.97×): 1.4–1.7× at 2048, 1.2–2.2× at 4096 and 1.46–3.1× at 8192 lit, up to 4.5× unlit. At 8192 copies the LOD mesh runs 48–83 FPS, Nanite with the game's settings 118–163 FPS.

The LOD side is limited by the render thread, not by triangles. At 8192 copies its render-thread time equals the frame time in every view with tables in it (12–21 ms), the far view and the unlit pass included, while Nanite's render thread stays at 3.5–6.2 ms. This is the per-primitive CPU cost (visibility, LOD selection, mesh draw command setup) the original article found in MeshDrawCommandPassSetupTask.

With few objects, the LOD mesh wins. A single table renders at 211–247 FPS as LOD against 185–212 FPS as Nanite (11–16 % slower): Nanite has a fixed cost of about 1 ms of GPU time on this GPU. The sky view, with almost nothing on screen, keeps LOD ahead at every count.

The game's Nanite settings beat the engine defaults in all 40 view and count combinations, by 0.3–29 % (r.Nanite.MaxPixelsPerEdge=2 and r.Nanite.Culling.TwoPass=0 against 1 and on).

What this means for impostors: they would not fix the LOD side here. Its cost is per actor on the CPU, and an impostor is still one actor. The levers are instancing (ISM/HISM, HLOD) or Nanite. Impostors help when the GPU is busy with far, dense geometry, which is not the case in this test.

Method

  • Flights. One packaged game run per flight, order LOD, Nz, Ns, Ns, Nz, LOD: every arm twice, at both ends, so drift shows up as spread. Each flight takes 6.7 min; 4 min rest between flights keeps the handheld out of its thermal and power limits.
  • Per segment. For each count and view: camera cut, 3 s settle (at least 5 frames), 5 s measured. Garbage collection is held off inside measured windows. FPS = 1000 / median frame time.
  • Counters. Frame, game-thread, render-thread and RHI times from the engine timers, GPU time from the last finished GPU frame, draw calls from the RHI. Two flights per arm; the median is shown and the spread is the difference between them (median 0.05 ms, 90 % of segments under 0.22 ms, worst 1.33 ms).
  • Validity. A flight counts only if the game kept the foreground the whole time, the display stayed 1280×800, the device stayed on AC, no other GPU work ran, every requested console variable was confirmed in the log and every copy was a Nanite proxy in the Nanite arms (none in the LOD arm). All 6 flights passed.
  • Unlit. Lighting off but the normal draw path kept (UE's own Unlit view mode would switch classic meshes to a per-frame debug path while Nanite ignores it).
  • Not covered. Instanced layouts (ISM/HISM, the bench supports -NLBLayout=ISM), masked or deforming materials, Nanite Foliage, virtual shadow maps, other GPUs.

Source, configs, tools and every flight's CSV/JSON are in the repository. The Megascans asset itself is not redistributed.
